I oppose the motion.

More laws and rules sound reassuring, but in practice they would likely slow innovation, entrench big tech, and do little to stop harm. LLMs are a fast-moving, global technology. If governments rush to regulate them heavily, the first companies to survive will be the largest ones with the money to comply. That means regulation can actually reduce competition and make AI less open, less affordable, and more concentrated in the hands of a few giants.

Worse, many proposed rules would be outdated before they are enforced. LLMs improve too quickly for rigid legal frameworks. By the time a law is written, debated, and implemented, the technology it targets will already have changed. That leaves us with expensive bureaucracy and little real protection. The better approach is flexible, case-by-case enforcement of existing laws—fraud, defamation, privacy, consumer protection—rather than creating a new layer of AI-specific restrictions.

The idea that regulation automatically creates safety is also false. Harm comes from misuse, not just the model itself. A scammer, a liar, or a negligent company can already do damage with or without an LLM. What matters is holding bad actors accountable under existing law, not burdening everyone with broad rules that punish legitimate research, education, and innovation.

Finally, overregulation risks pushing development underground or overseas, where there is even less oversight. If responsible firms are trapped by red tape, less responsible actors will fill the gap. That is the opposite of public protection.

So the real choice is not between safety and chaos. It is between smart, targeted enforcement and clumsy overregulation. For that reason, there should not be more laws and rules to regulate LLMs.
